        <p class="project-abstract">The rapid development of AI has left many of us with a particular sensation of losing control over our own futures. Will the efforts we invest now pay off in a few years? Will human intelligence even be necessary? For many, myself included, these questions remain simply unfathomable. In this work, I link this helplessness to the justificatory deterministic narratives that national policies use to push AI development and adoption: AI is inevitable, it is necessary, and hence it requires leadership to govern it properly. But is this narrative convincing to the public? Where does it break in public discourse? And when it is contested, does the contestation reach the inevitability premise itself, or does it simply question the visible leadership roles?</p>
        <p class="project-abstract">To examine how the public endorses and contests these policy narratives, I analyze discourse on Reddit. Across the two largest technology discussion forums on Reddit, I find a shift in perspectives: contestation against the inevitability premise grows after 2022, coinciding with the launch of ChatGPT. These findings open further questions. Is it the technology itself, or the recognition of leadership failure, that provokes people to contest? What responsibility do leaders bear for forfeiting our imaginative capabilities over our futures? And what does public contestation of AI inevitability claims imply for democratic decision-making?</p>

        <p class="project-abstract">The increased human-likeness of AI agents has intensified discussions on the perceived moral agency of AI, yet insufficient attention has been paid to the everyday language used to describe its actions. Addressing this gap, this study computationally analyzes anthropomorphic blame language in AI-related discussions on Reddit. Focusing on blame situations in the two largest AI-related subreddits, we explore the semantic similarities across blame language targeting AI, human, organizational, and societal actors.</p>
        <p class="project-abstract">Analysis of post-level embeddings reveals high overall semantic similarity across targets, with distinctive topical profiles. Blame directed at AI agents aligns closest to society-level blaming across topical anchors, while moral action anchors reveal structures unique to AI blaming. These findings suggest that AI is recognized as blameworthy, but the tone and style of its linguistic description are largely shared with other actors. We highlight the implications of these patterns in the public sensemaking of responsible AI design and governance.</p>
